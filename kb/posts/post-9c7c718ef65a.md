## Summary

This document analyzes the daily returns of the NIFTY 50 index from 1998 to 2015, highlighting distinct calendar-based patterns in market performance. The findings indicate that the initial and final trading days of a month present the most favorable conditions for long positions, whereas mid-month returns tend to be more volatile and negative on average. Furthermore, the text details a specific trading strategy that goes long during the first and last five days of the month while shorting the remaining days, yielding a cumulative return significantly higher than a traditional buy and hold strategy. The document also introduces related concepts such as strategy back-testing, path dependency, and the application of Monte Carlo simulations to validate trading strategies against historical price paths.

## Article

An analysis of daily returns of the NIFTY 50 index between 1998 and 2015 shows that: - The first few and the last few trading days of a month are the best days to be long. - Middle of the month returns are more volatile and, on an average, negative. - Cumulative returns of a strategy that is long on the first 5 and the last 5 days of the month and short the others is 1,112.154% vs. 636.1821% of a buy and hold strategy. [Linked: A Brief Note on Monte Carlo | https://stockviz.biz/2016/05/23/a-brief-note-on-monte-carlo/]
When we back-test a strategy against the historical prices of an instrument, say, the NIFTY 50 index, we have to keep in mind that historical values are just one path of the many paths that the instrument could have taken. For example, 10 tosses of a fair coin can result in TTTTTFFFFF and TFTFTFTFTF with equal probability. If your strategy is path dependent (as most strategies are,) then just because it was successful in one trial (historical prices) doesn’t mean that it would have been successful in all (or majority) of them. The simple thing to do after a successful back-test against historical prices is to run a Monte Carlo simulation to check if the strategy comes out ahead in most of them. This can be setup by assuming returns are normally distributed and running a simulation using the mean and standard deviation of the sample. For instance, in the recent past, daily NIFTY 50 returns have exhibited a mean of -0.0003742873 and std. dev. of 0.01079387. When you run a simulation and plot the results over the actual closing prices of the index, you get the resulting chart: How many of these paths will result in a total equity wipeout of the back-tested strategy?
