## Summary

This article discusses the integration of market sentiment analysis from news sources into risk-management models, specifically for adjusting stop-loss thresholds based on prevailing market conditions. StockViz approaches turning points in markets through a risk-management framework rather than pure prediction, noting that risk management inherently involves costs like hedging or stop-losses. By incorporating sentiment lexicons to gauge whether markets are extremely bullish or bearish, traders can dynamically widen or tighten stop-loss levels to reduce risk-management expenses. To test this hypothesis, StockViz established a weekly sentiment roundup across popular news sources to create a time series evaluating the correlation between sentiment metrics and subsequent market returns.

## Article

Markets trend, until they don’t. Markets consolidate until they start trending again. Since the beginning of markets, traders have been trying to figure out when these turning points occur. At StockViz, we have approached this problem from a risk-management angle rather than a prediction angle. We assume that we cannot predict, but we can definitely prepare. Risk-management, however, is not free. Whether you hedge or employ stop-losses, there is a cost involved. What if we can reduce this cost by employing market sentiment as an input to our risk-management models? One approach could be to widen stop-loss levels when the market is “extremely bearish” and tighten them when they get “extremely bullish.” To get things started, we have setup a weekly sentiment roundup that looks at a few popular news sources ranked using different lexicons. The first step would be create a time series of these values to see how they relate to market returns. Stay tuned!

Traditional equity momentum strategies are variations of algos that try to figure out “trending” stocks so that they can be ranked to create a long/short portfolio. The key thing to remember is that these algos are following a trend, the prediction that a trending stock will continue to trend is implicit. However, using machine learning techniques, stocks can be ranked based on their predicted returns over a future time frame. The simplest momentum strategy looks only at a price series. However, it quickly runs into problems when additional factors are overlaid on top of basic momentum. For example, you may want to filter out volatile stocks out the basket. You can do this either by setting a maximum volatility level or by weighing both momentum and volatility to arrive at a combined rank. Either approach leads to ad hoc decisions of cut off levels and the ratio with which to weigh each of those factors. Luckily, typical machine learning algorithms can work with multiple factors and weigh them based on the training set you supply. The biggest drawback of using machine learning is that the larger the number of factors/features you use, the less explainable the resulting model becomes. As a trader, if you want to use any of these models, you should have a fairly good idea of what is going in, how the model is setup and what exactly is the model getting trained with. As a first step in taking a crack at this, we have setup four machine learning algos. Two of them use SVRs and the other two use LR to train on data that is either return-series only or a combination of returns and volatility. You can have look at them here.
