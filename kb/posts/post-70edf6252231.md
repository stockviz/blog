# Reinforcement Learning for Finance

## Summary

This specialist research program tests various reinforcement learning agents on Indian stocks and futures data to determine if they can outperform classical momentum and trend baselines net of trading costs. Across multiple rewards, states, actions, algorithms, universes, and venues, no RL agent successfully beat the classical baselines out-of-sample. The program identifies key market structures, such as the negative carry of intraday NIFTY trading compared to overnight gains, and categorizes RL failure modes like drift-betting, churn, and flat-optimality. The research concludes that reinforcement learning fails to provide a profitable edge over simpler systems, with its only notable reproducible contribution being drawdown avoidance.

## Article

We combined books and research papers to create a knowledge base. We then used that to create a research program to check if they can be applied to Indian stocks/futures for profit. Our threshold for success was outperformance against naive momentum/trend strategies. RL implementations tend to be complicated and compute intensive. They least they can do is beat simple systems. Also, India is an expensive venue to trade so these tests included those costs as well. Across every reward, cost, state, action, algorithm, universe, and venue we tried, no RL agent beat classical baselines net of costs out-of-sample. Our research plan, and eli5 are good places to start. Or, you can jump right into the summary of the results. This is by no means exhaustive and we chose simple RL systems to get our beaks wet. However, the lack of WOW! is concerning. Code, charts, etc. are on github.

There was a robot named Captain Q. Captain Q's one dream was to learn how to make money by trading — buying things when they are cheap and selling them when they are expensive. Trading is like a game. Every morning the shops open, and the price of everything wiggles up and down all day. If you buy a toy for 10 pennies and it goes up to 12 pennies, you made 2 pennies! If it goes down to 8 pennies, you lost 2 pennies. Simple! But there is a sneaky rule: every time you buy or sell, you have to pay the shopkeeper 5 tiny pennies. So you can't just buy and sell all day — the shopkeeper eats your money. You have to be smart about WHEN to buy and WHEN to sell. Captain Q wanted to be the smartest trader in the world. So he went to practice on the biggest, most famous mountain of toys in India: the NIFTY 50 Mountain. On that mountain live 50 big toy companies, and their prices all go up and down together like one giant wobbly tower. He also met the other traders who live there: - Turtle Tim — slow and steady. Turtle Tim has a simple rule: if the mountain has been going UP, he stays and plays. If it has been going DOWN, he sits on the grass and watches. He never gets greedy. - Elephant Ella — she buys her favorite toys and then... just holds them. Forever. She never sells, no matter what! (This works great when everything keeps going up.) - Raccoon Randy — he just does random things. He buys when he feels like it, sells when he feels like it. Nobody listens to Randy. Captain Q practiced on the mountain using make-believe days that looked like real days (grown-ups call this "artificial trajectories" — it means he practiced on pretend replays so he wouldn't waste real days). First problem: Captain Q traded TOO MUCH. He would buy, sell, buy, sell, buy, sell — and every time he did, the shopkeeper took 5 pennies. Pretty soon the shopkeeper had all of Captain Q's money! The grown-ups measured it: Captain Q was paying almost half his money every year in shopkeeper fees. Ouch! Second problem: Even when Captain Q learned to calm down and hold his toys like Elephant Ella, he still wasn't better than Turtle Tim. Turtle Tim's simple rule was just... better. Every time Captain Q tried something new — new tricks, new rules, new ways of looking at the mountain — Turtle Tim still won. The grown-ups tried everything: - They tried giving Captain Q a different reward (he likes the game best when he's told "make money, but not too wobbly"). - They tried making the shopkeeper cheaper (5 pennies instead of 25). - They tried showing Captain Q scary-weather signs (the "VIX umbrella" and the "risk-off fog" — signs that a storm is coming). - They tried letting him pick toys from a whole shelf of 900 companies. - They tried making him hold his toys with a leash so he couldn't jump around (this actually helped!). - They even tried letting Captain Q decide how much of his money to put in each toy basket every week. And do you know what the BEST thing Captain Q ever did was? He learned how to not lose money. When the mountain fell, Captain Q hid in his fort (grown-ups call it "drawdown avoidance" — his biggest ouchie was only 9 pennies out of 100, while Turtle Tim's was 16 and Elephant Ella's was 25). But hiding in a fort all the time means you don't FIND any candy either. Captain Q kept his money safe, but he couldn't make it grow like Turtle Tim. The grown-ups wrote it all down: Captain Q is a very good hider, but Turtle Tim and Elephant Ella are better money-makers. The grown-ups had another idea. The mountain's shops are open from 9:15 in the morning to 3:29 in the afternoon. What if Captain Q traded INSIDE the day — buying in the morning and selling in the afternoon, using tiny one-minute ticks like a metronome? So Captain Q practiced on every single minute of the day. He met a new friend: the Night Monster. The Night Monster is very important. It turns out that the Mountain makes almost ALL of its money AT NIGHT — while everyone is asleep, the prices quietly creep up. And during the DAY, the mountain actually LOSES a little bit of money — it leaks! The grown-ups measured it: if you buy the mountain in the morning and sell it in the afternoon, every single day, for six years... you lose 15 pennies out of 100 every year! The daytime is a leaky bucket. The money is made in the dark. That's like trying to catch fish in a swimming pool that's being drained. You can be the best fisher-robot in the world — there are no fish! So Captain Q went somewhere else. He went to the Gold Playground (MCX — where they trade shiny gold). TL;DR — We built a rigorous RL-for-finance research program on the StockViz data estate (daily + 1-minute Indian market data), executed it phase-by-phase against a written plan with house backtesting conventions, and produced a fully documented NEGATIVE: across every reward, cost, state, action, algorithm, universe, and venue we tried, no RL agent beat classical baselines net of costs out-of-sample. The program's real outputs are durable market-structure findings (NIFTY-family intraday is negative-carry; returns live in overnight gaps), a taxonomy of RL failure modes (drift-betting, flat-optimality, churn), and a reproducible pipeline to test the next idea. Deployment verdict: no. This document tells that story the way engineers like it: as a narrative with the numbers intact, the plot twists documented, and the receipts in research/phase1/runs/*. Everything lives under /mnt/data/books/RL/: - kb/ — an 11-file knowledge base distilled from 8 sources (Hilpisch's RL-for-Finance book, Théate & Ernst TDQN, Si et al. intraday LSTM, Ponomarev A3C, Hambly et al. survey, ...) with a frequency matrix. - research-plan.md — the master plan: Phase 0 (infrastructure) → Phase 1 (daily [CLOSE]) → Phase 2 (intraday [INTRADAY]) → Phase 3 (frontier), with M0–M4 milestones, exit gates, and an honest-outcome clause (negative results are legitimate conclusions). - research/phase0/ — the infrastructure: a testedrl package (39/39 unit tests) with data loaders (daily SQL Server + intraday Postgres, per-table tick-epoch decoders), six Gym environments, four agent families (DQN/TDQN, Si-LSTM BPTT, SB3 PPO/A2C), house metrics/charts, and a run registry mirroring the R2 tree'srun-all.R . - research/phase1/ — the experiments, oneruns/<id>/ directory each, every one with checkpoints, params.json, metrics CSVs, house charts (stacked cum+drawdown, end-labeled, @StockViz), and a findings.md. House conventions held throughout: pre ≤ 2019-12-31 / post ≥ 2020-05-01, costs in-reward, no-lookahead (state at t, reward on r_{t+1}), per-day (never per-bar pooled) intraday statistics, fixed seeds, and a classical baseline zoo (random, buy-and-hold, MA20/50) as the floor every agent must clear. Replicated TDQN (Double+Dueling, differential-Sharpe reward) on NIFTY 50 daily with S4-style artificial-trajectory training (2000–2014), tested 2015+. Verdict: catastrophic churn — 177–222 position flips/year at 25bps ≈ 45–55%/year drag; full-window Sharpe -2.08 vs MA +0.76 and B&H +0.64. Repeated on NIFTY MIDCAP SELECT with identical failure → the mechanism, not the data, was broken. Five arms isolating reward and cost: differential-Sharpe churns at any cost (confirmed broken as an immediate reward); PnL @5bps and churn-penalized PnL @25bps learn to HOLD (Sharpe -2.08 → +0.19/+0.30). The mechanism fix worked; the floor (beat MA net of costs) still failed by d = -0.55. VIX z/change + TVT-HMM p_off as state: +0.76 Sharpe ablation vs the control, and the best post-2020 window result of the daily track — the regime features deliver exactly the de-risking information the agent lacked. Still no floor crossing. One shared TDQN over a top-50 FF universe looked POSITIVE (book +1.21 vs MA +1.14, 94% names positive). Plot twist: widening to the FF60 universe (970 names) exposed it as drift-betting — the pnl-reward agent learns the training window's best CONSTANT direction. The 4-attempt collapse taxonomy (runs/r15_ff60/findings.md): under-coverage (always-short), drift-betting (always-short in a bear slice, always-flat in the long-only corner), and finally a stable-but-edge-free policy after de-drifted trajectories + per-stock vol-scaled rewards + long-only actions. [Linked: Stop-losses or Stop-profits? | https://stockviz.biz/2026/08/23/stop-losses-or-stop-profits/]
While we use trailing stop losses in our Rapid Fire themes, they are by no means the brahmastra they are made out to be. We audited two of the strategies in that theme: Momo (Velocity) v1.0 and Momo (Relative) v1.1. We would have made more money by doing nothing. Some findings (details): - The stock that was sold usually went up after. At 20-days, you were 1.5× more likely to miss a >5% rally than to avoid a >5% slide. - The stock that was bought instead did about the same. A drift-positive basket was replaced with another drift-positive basket. The churn bought nothing and you paid transaction costs on top. Trailing stop losses might be psychological crutches. But with everything else in finance that is designed to ease the pain of ownership, you end up paying for it. Analysis, charts and code are on github.
