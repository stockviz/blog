## Summary

This document explores the critiques and limitations of technical analysis in financial markets, citing insights from Michael Harris and other financial commentators. It argues that price movements precede technical indicators, support levels, and resistance lines, making descriptive technical analysis largely useless unless combined with a measurable probability of events. The article references studies showing that retail traders relying on technical analysis often incur substantial financial losses, and questions the statistical validity of tools like Fibonacci retracements. Additionally, it compares these findings with broader perspectives on quantitative variables having minor predictive validity, highlighting the complexities and frequent shortcomings of relying solely on charts and indicators for trading decisions.

## Article

Michael Harris over at Price Action Lab: Saying that “if these levels hold prices will rise and if they do not, prices will fall”, is a statement that confuses event order. Price moves first and then we decide whether some level or indicator was violated. If a level does not hold, it is because price already moved below it or above it. It is not the other way around. In technical analysis, use of any derivative of price, including support and resistance levels, indicators or patterns, is often useless, unless there is a measure of the probability of some event. Otherwise, just describing these levels is either only stating the facts or it is naive analysis due to ignorance.
