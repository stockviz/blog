# Your Friends may be your Portfolio’s worst Enemy

## Summary

This article explores how social influence and peer behavior heavily impact financial investment decisions, showing that individuals often chase hot market trends because people around them are doing the same. Citing research by Nobel Prize-winning economist Robert Shiller, the text highlights that the majority of both individual and institutional investors make choices driven by group attention and peer discussions rather than systematic research. Furthermore, the psychological concept of group attention is examined to explain how shared experiences amplify emotions such as fear, gloom, and glee, which subsequently leads to heightened emotional reactions in financial markets and potential detriment to investment portfolios.

## Article

Consider this: - People who live near each other increase their ownership of stocks around the same time. - 401(k) investors put more money into stocks when their co-workers have recently earned high returns that way. - Mutual-fund managers are more likely to invest in a company when other stock-pickers in the same city are also buying it. Nobel Prize-winning economist Robert Shiller found that for 62% of individual investors and a shocking 75% of institutional investors, the decision to invest in stocks with hot recent returns had nothing to do with a “systematic search.” Instead, these investors seemed to chase whatever grabbed their attention the most because other people were talking about it. And it is not only in investing. “Group attention” – the experience of simultaneous co-attention with one’s group members – increases emotional intensity relative to attending alone. Greater fear, gloom, and glee is a result from group attention to scary, sad, and happy events, respectively. Individuals come to feel more when they are together. Sources:
